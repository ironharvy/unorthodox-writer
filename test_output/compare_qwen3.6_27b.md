# qwen3.6:27b

The gravel crunched under Elara’s boots, a sound too loud for the silence pressing against the house. She had expected the familiar geometry of her father’s work: rows of apple trees spaced with military precision, their trunks whitewashed, their branches pruned into obedient arcs. Instead, the air was thick with the scent of damp rot and ancient pine.

She stepped off the driveway, her hand brushing the rough bark of a tree that shouldn’t exist. It was massive, its roots bulging like knuckles from the earth, strangling the old fence line. The orchard was gone. In its place stood a dense, shadowed thicket, the canopy so thick it blotted out the afternoon sun, casting the yard in a premature, bruised twilight.

Elara walked forward, the undergrowth snagging at her jeans. The air felt heavier here, charged with the static of rapid, impossible growth. She reached for the first tree she recognized—a Gala, planted the year she turned twelve. But the trunk was too wide, the leaves too dark, veined with a purple hue that looked almost bruised.

She pushed deeper, heart hammering a frantic rhythm against her ribs. The ground was soft, spongy with moss that hadn’t been there yesterday. Then, she saw it. Half-buried in the loam, near the base of a twisted oak, was her father’s trowel. The wooden handle was smooth, worn by years of grip, but the metal blade was rusted shut, fused to a root that pulsed faintly, as if with a slow, subterranean heartbeat.

She didn’t pick it up. To touch it felt like interrupting a prayer.

Elara turned back toward the house, the windows dark and uninviting. The forest did not rustle; it watched. She realized then that the trees were not growing around the house, but into it, their roots seeking the foundation, the memory, the bone. She left the trowel in the dirt. Some things, she understood now, were not meant to be harvested, only endured. She walked back to her car, the engine’s hum the only human sound left in a world that had decided to forget them.
